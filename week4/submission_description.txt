This Week 4 report designs a conceptual prototype for a new biotechnological product based on the prior research and experimental insights developed across the earlier components of this internship, and documents the development process, the underlying theory, the expected performance, the necessary materials and methods, the risk assessment and the potential improvements, covering every requirement of the task. The prototype is a modular, transportable bioprocess unit that converts food waste into polyhydroxyalkanoate bioplastic at the point where the waste is generated. It comprises four modules: an acidogenesis module that converts food waste into a volatile fatty acid liquor by dark fermentation with the pH held at 5.2 so that methanogens are inhibited and carboxylic acids accumulate instead of biogas; a fed-batch accumulation module in which engineered Cupriavidus necator converts that liquor into intracellular polymer; a recovery module that removes non-PHA biomass by enzymatic digestion rather than by chlorinated solvent; and a shared control and data layer. The design's central mechanism is a pH-static control loop that measures residual acid continuously and adds feed only as fast as the culture consumes it, holding the reactor near one gram per litre while the feed vessel holds a concentrated stream, which converts the process's hardest problem from a batch issue into a control problem and fails safe if the sensor drifts. Detailed sketches and diagrams illustrate how the unit functions, including a system architecture drawing, a sectional view of the bioreactor, the control loop, the process flow and its mass balance, the risk matrix and the development roadmap. The theoretical performance assessment projects about one point one kilograms of dry polymer powder per day from roughly twenty kilograms of dry food waste, an overall mass yield of five to six per cent, with the reasoning set out and every figure identified as a projection from published yields rather than a measured result. Twelve failure modes are scored for likelihood and impact before mitigation, and the analysis shows the highest-scoring risks are economic and biological rather than technical. Six potential improvements are each paired with the evidence behind them and the cost they would introduce, and a four-stage development plan with stated stop conditions completes the document.
